Abstract  Forensic sketch analysis remains a critical resource in investigative workflows, particularly in situations where video or photographic evidence is unavailable or insufficient.   However, eyewitness-generated sketches often capture only key facial features, vary widely in quality, and lack the texture and color necessary for accurate recognition.   Conventional comparison techniques between sketches and photographs are labor-intensive and depend heavily on human expertise, making early-stage matching slow and prone to inconsistencies. In this work, we propose a multimodal sketch-to-face generative system that integrates visually based sketches with text-based descriptions to automatically generate realistic facial images. This system is designed to capture both the structural outlines provided by the witness sketch and the semantic characteristics described verbally. Our approach leverages a CLIP-guided Conditional GAN architecture.   Descriptive at- tributes such as race, gender, hair type, skin tone, and eye color are encoded into high-level textual embeddings using the CLIP ViT-B/32 encoder. These semantic cues complement the sketch by providing information that simple line drawings cannot convey. Simultaneously, the input sketch and its corresponding mask are processed through a multi-stage encoder, extract- ing hierarchical features ranging from edges to overall facial structure. At the bottleneck, visual and textual features undergo fusion and refinement, ensuring that the generated face adheres to the sketch’s geometry while reflecting the semantic attributes. The fused representation is then decoded into a high-resolution image, with skip connections preserving important contours and facial layout. A PatchGAN discriminator further enhances realism by evaluating local image patches and encouraging sharper, more lifelike outputs. By integrating structure from sketches and meaning from textual descriptions, the system produces reconstructions that are more accurate, consistent with witness accounts, and ulti- mately more useful for forensic analysis. Overall, this multimodal framework reduces manual workload, improves sketch-based identification, and provides investigators with rapid and ef- fective suspect visualization. It paves the way for more reliable forensic technologies, especially in scenarios involving incomplete or limited visual evidence. 3

1   Introduction  In traditional investigations, sketches are drawn by skilled artists based on descriptions provided by eyewitnesses. These sketches serve as crucial visual aids in identifying suspects. However, sketches alone are often insufficient, as human perception is highly visual and context-driven. They lack key features such as skin tone, texture, and subtle facial expressions, making accu- rate identification difficult. Moreover, manually comparing forensic sketches with large mugshot databases is tedious, time-consuming, and prone to human error. A more realistic visual repre- sentation of the suspect significantly increases the likelihood of successful identification. Such sketch-to-image mappings can also be stored in centralized databases and shared across inves- tigative agencies for cross-referencing, especially when suspects are involved in crimes across multiple regions.   Additionally, these databases can serve as valuable references in unsolved cases where suspects remain unidentified. The entire identification process can be automated with the help of Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs). Incorporating textual attributes such as gender, skin tone, and hair type can make reconstructions more precise and semantically aligned with witness descriptions, thereby assisting law enforcement agencies in more efficient and accurate suspect identification. We propose a CLIP-guided Conditional GAN for sketch-to-face synthesis. The model ex- tracts textual embeddings from descriptive inputs using CLIP and fuses them with encoded sketch features in an encoder-decoder generator architecture.   The discriminator refines the generated results through adversarial learning. By combining adversarial, L1, and CLIP-based perceptual losses, the model produces high-resolution, semantically consistent facial images that align with both the structural details of the sketch and the descriptive textual cues, thereby offering a practical, automated solution for modern forensic applications. According to reports from various agencies like the FBI and numerous police departments of India, nearly 25–40% of early-stage investigations still rely on forensic sketches created from eyewitness ac- counts. These are usually suffering from serious limitations, though. Because witnesses remem- ber faces during stress or much later, the drawings tend to be rough, incomplete, or inaccurate. As a matter of fact, forensic studies have indicated that 70–80% of the sketches miss the es- sential visual details such as shadings, textures, and symmetry that make them difficult to compare against real photographs. This lack of detail leads to another major challenge:   the traditional analysis of sketches relies almost solely on line drawings, without taking into consideration any other information that the officer may collect on skin tone, age, race, hairstyle, and other identifying attributes. Highlighted in a 2024 article in The Hindu, police often record these details when interview- ing witnesses, but most current AI tools don’t use them.   The result is that today’s systems 7

often spit out images with mistaken identity cues-skin color that doesn’t match, wrong facial proportions, or a hair pattern that’s off. A third and equally serious problem is the lack of demographic diversity in available datasets. Most of the older models have been trained on datasets with a mainly Western bias, where there is limited variation in skin tone or ethnic facial structure. Investigative reports from India, the UK, and Southeast Asia mention time and again how these systems malfunction when dealing with locals, for whom their training data is unrepresentative.   This, in turn, yields biased or unrealistic outputs, making it difficult for police to rely on such models during active cases. Incomplete sketches, lacking semantic information, and the absence of demographic diversity compound on each other to make the reconstruction of realistic and identity-preserving faces from forensic sketches extremely challenging.   What appears conspicuous in its absence is a system that integrates information from several sources instead of relying on the sketch for a meaningful and reliable reconstruction that is useful in real-world forensic applications.   Our project addresses this need by introducing a multimodal approach to sketch-to-face generation, merging together the structure of sketches and spatial cues with textual descriptions to overcome current system limitations.  1.2   Objectives  The objective of this research is to design and implement a Generative Adversarial Network (GAN)-based framework capable of transforming forensic or hand-drawn sketches into realistic facial images that closely resemble actual photographs.   The aim is to preserve key identity- defining features such as facial structure, proportions, and expressions while enhancing overall image quality, texture, and visual clarity.   This model seeks to assist forensic experts and law enforcement agencies in the process of suspect identification by bridging the gap between artistic sketches and real-world facial representations.  1.3   Scope  The scope includes designing a Dual-Path Encoder–Decoder GAN, integrating three modali- ties—sketch, spatial mask, and text description—and incorporating CLIP-based semantic con- ditioning, self-attention refinement, and eye-focused detail enhancement. The system is trained and evaluated using paired sketch–photo datasets and supports extensible conditioning for di- verse facial attributes. its dynamic computation graphs, modular structure, and native support for GPU acceleration. Textual descriptions are processed using CLIP ViT-B/32 model. To address this, HiTS introduces hierarchical textual conditioning using intrinsic and mutable attributes.  2.1.2   Methodology  The HiTS model integrates multiple innovations:  •   Intrinsic vs.   Mutable Attribute Separation:   Text descriptions are divided into intrinsic attributes (race, gender) and mutable attributes (skin tone, hair color, eye color). This enables hierarchical stylization at global and local levels. 9

•   Cross-Attention Encoder:   A cross-attention module aligns visual feature maps and intrinsic attribute embeddings to extract global identity features.  •   Semantic-Textual Embedding Map (STEM):   A novel embedding map assigns local- ized text embeddings to each facial component, guided by a parsing mask. The analysis encompasses system architecture, data flow representations, and operational workflows that illustrate how multimodal inputs are processed through the deep learning pipeline to generate realistic facial images.  3.1   System Architecture  The system architecture consists of three primary components operating in a coordinated man- ner: the Input Processing Pipeline, the Generator Network with CLIP-guided conditioning, and the PatchGAN Discriminator with adversarial training loop. Figure 1 illustrates the high-level architecture showing the flow of data from raw inputs through preprocessing, feature extraction, multimodal fusion, and image synthesis stages. The architecture implements a dual-pathway design where visual features from sketches and spatial masks are processed through convolutional encoders, while textual descriptions are transformed into semantic embeddings via CLIP. These modalities converge at the bottle- neck layer where cross-modal fusion occurs through element-wise addition and self-attention mechanisms. 13

Figure 1: High-level system architecture showing the three main components and their inter- actions  3.1.1   Input Processing Pipeline  Raw sketch images (RGB format) and mask images (grayscale) undergo standardized prepro- cessing. Images are resized to 256 × 256 resolution, normalized to the range [-1, 1], and converted to PyTorch tensors. Textual descriptions are tokenized using the OpenCLIP tokenizer (maxi- mum 77 tokens) and encoded into 512-dimensional dense vectors through the CLIP ViT-B/32 text encoder. The sketch (3 channels) and mask (1 channel) are concatenated along the channel dimension to form a 4-channel multimodal visual input tensor.  3.1.2   Generator Network  The concatenated visual input passes through four hierarchical convolutional encoding blocks, each applying 4 × 4 convolutions with stride 2, progressively reducing spatial dimensions while increasing feature depth (64   →   128   →   256   →   512 channels).   The final encoder output un- dergoes dual-pooling (adaptive max and average pooling) to create complementary feature representations.   The 1024-channel pooled features are reduced to 256 channels through se- quential convolutional layers, while the 512-dimensional CLIP text embedding is projected and reshaped into a spatial feature map (256 × 32 × 32). These visual and textual features are fused via element-wise addition, followed by multi-head self-attention (4 heads) to model long-range dependencies. Three progressive upsampling blocks with skip connections reconstruct the final 256 × 256 RGB image.  3.1.3   Discriminator Network  The PatchGAN discriminator receives concatenated inputs (sketch, mask, and either real or generated photo) totaling 7 channels. It processes these through five spectral-normalized convo- lutional layers, outputting patch-wise predictions (16 × 16) to evaluate local realism rather than global image authenticity. Spectral normalization ensures training stability by constraining the 14

Lipschitz constant of the discriminator.  3.2   Data Flow Analysis  Figure 2 presents the detailed data flow through the system, tracking the transformation of inputs from their raw formats to the final generated facial image. The diagram identifies six distinct processing stages: data ingestion, preprocessing, feature encoding, multimodal fusion, image synthesis, and adversarial discrimination. The encoding stage extracts hierarchical visual features through the generator’s encoder while CLIP produces text embeddings.   The fusion stage combines these modalities at the bottleneck. 3.3   Training Workflow  The training process follows an adversarial learning paradigm with alternating optimization of generator and discriminator networks.   Figure 3 illustrates the complete training workflow including batch processing, forward propagation, loss computation, and backpropagation steps.  Figure 3:   Training workflow showing the adversarial learning cycle between Generator and Discriminator  3.3.1   Discriminator Training Phase  In each iteration, the discriminator receives two types of inputs:   real pairs (sketch, mask, ground-truth photo) and fake pairs (sketch, mask, generated photo).   The discriminator pro- cesses these through spectral-normalized convolutional layers and outputs patch-wise predic- tions. Binary cross-entropy loss is computed separately for real samples (target=1) and fake samples (target=0), and the averaged loss guides discriminator weight updates through the Adam optimizer with learning rate 1 × 10.  3.3.2   Generator Training Phase  The generator training phase incorporates multiple loss components to ensure high-fidelity, semantically consistent outputs. The weighted combination includes:  •   Adversarial Loss   (=1):   Encourages the generator to produce images that fool the discriminator  •   L1 Reconstruction Loss   (=50): Ensures pixel-level similarity with ground truth through mean absolute error  •   Perceptual Loss   (=20):   Uses VGG-19 features (up to layer 18) to match high-level semantic content in deep feature space  •   Edge Loss   (=5):   Applies Sobel gradient filters to preserve structural boundaries and high-frequency contours 16

•   Total Variation Loss   (=2):   Regularizes spatial smoothness to reduce checkerboard artifacts  •   Feature Matching Loss   (=10): Matches intermediate discriminator feature activations between real and fake samples  •   Eye-Focused Loss   (=90 combined): When MediaPipe face mesh is available, extracts eye regions (64 × 64 patches) and applies L1, perceptual, and Laplacian sharpness losses for enhanced detail in identity-critical facial features The total generator loss is the weighted sum of all components.   Gradients are computed via backpropagation and weights are updated using the Adam optimizer.   Learning rates for both networks decay by a factor of 0.5 every 10 epochs through StepLR schedulers.   Model checkpoints are saved every 10 epochs, storing generator and discriminator state dictionaries along with optimizer and scheduler states to enable training resumption.  3.4   Class Structure  The implementation follows object-oriented principles with three primary classes:  3.4.1   SketchFaceDataset Class  Inherits from   torch.utils.data.Dataset   and implements custom data loading logic. Key at- tributes include file paths for sketches, photos, and masks, along with transformation pipelines. The   getitem   method loads image triplets and corresponding textual descriptions, ap- plies preprocessing transformations, and returns batched tensors (sketch: 3 × 256 × 256, mask: 1 × 256 × 256, photo: 3 × 256 × 256) along with text strings suitable for model training.  3.4.2   Generator Class  Implements the   nn.Module   interface with a dual-path encoder-decoder architecture containing approximately 15.4 million parameters. Sub-modules include four encoding blocks (enc1-enc4), dual pooling layers, fusion convolutions, text projection layers, multi-head self-attention mech- anism, three decoding blocks (dec1-dec3), and three residual adapters.   The forward method accepts sketch tensors, mask tensors, and 512-dimensional text embeddings, orchestrating the complete generation pipeline to produce 3 × 256 × 256 RGB output images.  3.4.3   Discriminator Class  A convolutional neural network with approximately 5.4 million parameters and spectral nor- malization applied to all convolutional layers for training stability.   The architecture consists of five blocks progressively increasing channel depth (64 → 128 → 256 → 512 → 1) while reducing 17

spatial resolution. It implements both standard forward propagation (for adversarial classifi- cation) and   forward features   method for extracting intermediate activations used in feature matching loss computation.  3.5   Dataset Analysis  The training dataset comprises 2,292 paired sketch-photo samples sourced from FS2K and CUHK datasets. 4.2   Input Processing Module  4.2.1   Dataset Composition  Each training instance is composed of the following modalities: 1.   Sketch Image (RGB):   A 3-channel sketch generated from original facial photos. Al- though visually grayscale, sketches are stored as RGB for compatibility with convolutional backbones. 2.   Ground Truth Photo (RGB):   The corresponding real face image used as the super- vision target. 3.   Mask Image (Grayscale):   A 1-channel full-white silhouette mask used as a spatial prior. If a mask is missing, a blank mask is automatically generated. 4.   Textual Description:   Sentence-level semantic attributes (e.g., “female with long brown hair and sharp eyebrows”). These descriptions are encoded using CLIP ViT-B/32.  4.2.2   Preprocessing  All images are resized to 256   ×   256, normalized to the range [ − 1 ,   1], and converted to ten- sors. Text descriptions are tokenized using the OpenCLIP tokenizer and encoded into a 512- dimensional representation. Sketch images (3 channels) and mask images (1 channel) are con- catenated to form a 4-channel multimodal input tensor.  4.3   Generator Network  The generator adopts a dual-path encoder–decoder architecture enhanced with multi-scale fea- ture fusion, textual conditioning, and self-attention refinement.   This design enables effective integration of structural, spatial, and semantic information for high-fidelity face synthesis.  4.3.1   Encoder Pathway  The encoder consists of four hierarchical convolutional blocks, each responsible for progressively abstracting spatial features while increasing channel depth. Each convolutional block uses a 4 × 4 convolution with stride 2, followed by Batch Normalization (applied in all blocks except Enc1), and a LeakyReLU activation function.   The encoder progressively reduces spatial resolution while enriching feature depth, enabling the network to capture both fine contours and coarse identity descriptors. 20

4.3.2   Dual-Pooling Feature Fusion  The final encoder output (Enc4) is processed through both Adaptive Max Pooling and Adaptive Average Pooling, producing two feature maps of size 512   ×   32   ×   32. These representations are concatenated channel-wise and passed through two convolutional layers to generate a fused 256   ×   32   ×   32 feature tensor.This fusion enhances representational richness at the bottleneck.  4.3.3   Textual Conditioning via CLIP Projection  The 512-dimensional text embedding produced by CLIP is first passed through a linear layer that reduces its dimensionality to 256, followed by a ReLU activation. It is then passed through a second linear layer to project it to a tensor of size 256   ×   32   ×   32. This resulting tensor is reshaped into a spatial semantic map of size 256   ×   32   ×   32 and added element-wise to the fused visual feature tensor.   By injecting these identity cues and descriptive attributes at the bottleneck level, the generator can produce images that faithfully reflect text-provided characteristics such as gender, hair style, or fine-grained facial details.  4.3.4   Self-Attention Refinement  A Self-Attention Refinement refines the fused tensor by capturing long-range dependencies across facial regions. This self-attention mechanism helps the generator maintain global struc- tural consistency and resolve interdependent features such as: Eye–eye symmetry, Facial align- ment, Global coherence of shape and shading, Illumination consistency.   By modeling these relationships, the network produces globally coherent and visually consistent output images.  4.3.5   Decoder and Residual Adapters  The decoder progressively downsamples the feature representation in three stages, integrating skip connections from corresponding encoder layers. Each skip connection is processed through a lightweight residual adapter implemented using a 1   ×   1 convolution. The residual adapters reintroduce low-level contours and edge information that may have been lost during downsam- pling in the encoder. Finally, a 3   ×   3 convolution followed by a   tanh   activation produces the normalized RGB output image.  4.4   Datasets Used  For training, we used a total of 2292 paired sketches and real face images collected from the FS2K and CUHK datasets.   Each pair consists of a sketch, which may be hand-drawn or algorithmically generated, aligned with its corresponding real photo. For each sample, a full- white silhouette mask is automatically generated to serve as a spatial prior.   Additionally, sentence-level textual descriptions were manually written for every sample in a consistent format 21

We found that the combination of the input sketch with semantic attributes enabled the system to generate faces that were similar to the overall structure of the drawing while reflecting skin tone, gender, and hair type features.   The model works quite well for most samples and preserves geometry based on a sketch rather effectively.   However, it still struggles with fine-grained features, such as scars or wrinkles, and heavily depends on how clear or complete the sketch is. Nonetheless, the system has great potential in helping forensic teams to quickly narrow down suspects or visualize leads where photographs are not available. In the future, this work can be extended by more diverse dataset which would improve the generalization capability of this model across different ethnic groups and age ranges. Automatic face alignment before training will improve consistency, especially for off-centered sketches or ones drawn by artists with different drawing styles.   We also believe that higher-resolution 26

generators or attention-based refinement networks are useful to capture finer facial details. Finally, it would be interesting to explore text-input flexibility, allowing natural sentences instead of structured descriptions, which would make this system easier for real forensic teams to use. In summary, though there is much to be done, this project provides a good foundation for a practical, scalable tool for sketch-to-face generation.  References  [1] Y. Choi, H. Kim, K. Sohn, and I.-J.
